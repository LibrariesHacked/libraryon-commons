**Libraries help us access fascinating material about the early years of circus — including [**rare 180 year-old posters**](https://www.flickr.com/photos/196656943@N03/albums/72177720325094282/), available to explore online for the first time.**

**Have you ever wondered where, why and how the circus began?**

Circus began as entertainment, but over decades and centuries became an artform.

Its early years contained fascinating characters and stories. The comedy of the ring would often contrast with real-life tragedy.

On this page you'll be able explore previously **unseen circus posters from the 1840s**, thanks to Wakefield Libraries. They're an example of how libraries can bring local and national history to life for everyone.

## Where the circus started

Philip Astley, a former sergeant major, is widely thought of as **the father of the modern circus**. He was born in 1742 in Newcastle-under-Lyme, Staffordshire.

A lover of horses from an early age, he joined Colonel Eliott's Light Horse regiment (also known as the 15th Light Dragoons) at the age of 17. By the time he ended his service he had developed advanced **trick horse-riding skills**.

In 1768 he headed to London and set up a riding school on land where, today, you’ll find Waterloo train station. Here he demonstrated his tricks, such as **standing on a horse’s back, dismounting and remounting, and standing on his head** – all while his horse cantered round in a circle. His wife, Patty, was also a trick rider and performed alongside him.

## From outdoor to amphitheatre

In refining his company's tricks, Astley set the size of the circle as 42 feet in diameter. These dimensions are still used as the standard circus ring size today.

Eventually Astley progressed from his open-air riding school and opened an **amphitheatre** for his shows.

In fact, he ended up having to build several venues in London alone, as they **kept burning down** – possibly due to the number of candles used to light the space.

Today, if you visit the garden at **St. Thomas’ Hospital** in London, you’ll find a plaque commemorating Philip Astley’s amphitheatre, which stood nearby.

## More acts added

To his own daring equestrianism, Astley eventually added other acts to his shows. These included jugglers, tightrope walkers and, in 1770, a clown known as ‘Mr Merryman’.

Astley also added **his son, John, as a performer**. Similarly skilled on horseback, he would go on to perform for **Marie Antoinette, the Queen of France**, in 1783. He would have been just 15 years old.

Astley junior’s performance left such an impression on the monarch that it helped his father gain permission to open an amphitheatre in Paris.

However, the French Revolution of 1789 led him to return to Britain, where he found competition from other circuses entertaining the masses...

## The importance of marketing

Increased competition meant that, as the 19th century dawned, circuses needed to put increased effort into **marketing themselves**.

A touring company would arrive at a new place and need to whip up enthusiasm for its limited number of shows before heading off on the road again.

To get the attention of potential audiences, circuses started getting **handbills and posters** printed. These would be handed out and pasted to walls throughout towns.

The paper used to print these materials would be finer than what we see newspapers printed on today. Advertising specific performances on specific dates, these 'bills' weren't intended to last long.

Even so, one Wakefield bookseller and stationer found them interesting enough to keep. His name was **John Cryer**.

John Cryer collected handbills, flyers, pamphlets and other ephemera in Wakefield in the 1800s. There was enough material to fill dozens of scrapbooks.

These scrapbooks are now part of [**Wakefield Libraries’ Cryer Collection**](https://www.twixtaireandcalder.org.uk/search?query=cryer), which offers a fascinating glimpse into life in the town in the 19th century.

The printers of these disposable artefacts would surely be very surprised to hear they’re still around over a century later...

:::hotspot{title="Cooke's Circus, Wakefield — 22 August 1844" x="17" y="7"}

Cooke's Circus was owned by the Cooke family, and they also performed prominently. On this date Henry Cooke and his brother James Cooke appear in several acts. There are also appearances by Master J. Cooke and Miss Cooke, presumably children from the Cooke family.

:::

:::hotspot{title="A benefit performance" x="65" y="22"}

In the 19th century dispensaries would provide medical care and medicines to the poor at low cost, or for free. This particular benefit performance appears to be for a local charitable cause.

:::

:::hotspot{title="The Cooke family" x="46" y="49"}

James and Henry Cooke's grandfather, Thomas Cooke, started Cooke's Circus in Scotland in the late 18th century. Many members and generations of the Cooke family performed in the circus — some seemingly as young as 18 months old.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Exotic acts" x="32" y="71"}

In the 19th century performers would use references to foreign lands and people to make their performances feel more exotic. These acts and references tend to feel inappropriate by today's standards.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Riding lessons" x="71" y="89"}

Performances also presented other revenue opportunities for circuses. Here we see that Henry Cooke, in addition to performing, offers daily riding lessons for local residents inspired by their experience.

:::

::::slide

![Historical archive image](/uploads/archive/cookes-circus-wakefield-poster-september-1844.jpg)

:::hotspot{title="Cooke's Circus, Wakefield — 7 September 1844" x="62" y="14"}

This performance takes place a couple of weeks after the previous poster date. It shows that even within the same run of shows, poster design — and the acts the posters billed — could vary considerably.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Benefit performances" x="66" y="23"}

'Benefit' performances meant popular individual performers would receive a greater share of ticket revenue. This was to reward them for potentially attracting more customers and increasing a circus company's business. Receiving the opportunity to 'take their benefit' meant the performer might also be more loyal to that circus owner.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Top billing" x="47" y="41"}

We see here that Mr. Seal's name appears three times before members of the Cooke family, who own the circus, are mentioned. This emphasises that on this date, he is the main draw. His talents are considered worthy of 'top billing'.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Mr Kite" x="37" y="61"}

The Mr Kite billed here is the same performer immortalised in The Beatles song, ['Being For the Benefit of Mr Kite!'](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bJVWZy4QOy0). It appears on their 1967 album, _Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band_.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Woodcut illustrations" x="38" y="87"}

To stand out against bills posted nearby, printers started incorporating woodcut illustrations. They would refer to the acts people could expect to see, often in an exaggerated or comical way.

:::

::::

::::slide

![Historical archive image](/uploads/archive/pablo-fanques-circus-poster-january-1847.jpg)

:::hotspot{title="Pablo Fanque's Circus, Wakefield — 25–27 January 1847" x="21" y="5"}

Pablo Fanque was born William Darby in Norwich in 1810. He was the first recorded Black circus owner in Britain. Pablo Fanque's Circus was popular across the country for several decades in the 19th century.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Susannah Darby" x="50" y="13"}

Mrs Pablo Fanque's name was Susannah Darby (born Susannah Marlaw). Just over a year on from this performance she would die tragically when a circus amphitheatre in Leeds collapsed under the weight of the crowd.

:::

:::hotspot{title="A ringmaster's introduction" x="11" y="19"}

In these early days of marketing, posters might use lengthy paragraphs of overblown language. It's possible circus owners were trying to copy a ringmaster’s excitable introduction, in which they try to whip up an audience’s anticipation.

:::

:::hotspot{title="A wordy passage" x="78" y="20"}

This wordy passage of text, which is all one sentence, can roughly be translated as: "Lots of people have enjoyed our performances, but we won't be here forever."

:::

:::hotspot{title="Timour the Tartar" x="34" y="50"}

_Timour the Tartar_ was written in 1811, and is often referenced as the first example of a ['hippodrama'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippodrama): a dramatic performance that prominently features horses. It's from this that we get the word 'hippodrome', which meant a venue that hosts horse-based entertainment. The writer of Timour the Tartar, Matthew Gregory Lewis, was also known as 'Monk' Lewis, because of the success of his salacious 1796 Gothic novel, [_The Monk_](https://blogs.bl.uk/english-and-drama/2016/09/the-monk-the-bible-and-obscenity.html).

:::

:::hotspot{title="Fire safety" x="61" y="94"}

The ban on smoking at performances is presumably a safety measure rather than a health-related one. With lots of wood used in early circus structures, fire was an ever-present danger.

:::

::::

::::slide

![Historical archive image](/uploads/archive/pablo-fanques-circus-poster-february-1-1847.jpg)

:::hotspot{title="Pablo Fanque's Circus, Wakefield — 1–3 February 1847" x="54" y="26"}

These performances take place just a few days after those in the previous Pablo Fanque poster. And yet it looks quite different, with more reliance on elegant illustrations of pageantry. There's a sense that circus owners may have been testing different poster designs to see which approach attracted most customers.

:::

:::hotspot{title="The bottle equilibrist" x="38" y="33"}

An 'equilibrist' was someone who could perform balancing acts, such as tightrope walking. A 'bottle equilibrist' would perform tricks while balancing on a bottle held between their feet! 'Amidio Neopert' (correct spelling likely 'Amidi Neoporte') is the figure depicted in [an 1846 Illustrated London News picture](https://www.lookandlearn.com/history-images/XJ101435/Amidi-Neuporte-the-great-Dutch-equilibrists-bottle-feat-at-Astleys?t=2&q=London&c=45&n=29910) showing a man at Astley's balancing on several bottles while spinning three plates.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Astley's Amphitheatre" x="64" y="66"}

Here we see it stated that the bottle equilibrist has previously performed for Queen Victoria and Prince Albert at Astley's Amphitheatre. While 'father of circus' Philip Astley had died decades prior, the London venue continued to bear his name.

:::

:::hotspot{title="A reference to indigenous people" x="65" y="73"}

In centuries past the term 'aborigine' was sometimes used to refer to indigenous people from British colonies. It's possible this misspelled usage refers to an act portraying [Tomochichi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomochichi), a prominent Native American figure from Georgia. It's also possible the act is confusing indigenous peoples from Australia with those from America.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Peking" x="52" y="88"}

Another misspelled instance is this reference to Peking (now Bejing), China and its people's 'eccentricities'. Given few 19th century circus owners would have visited China, it's likely these characterisations would have been rather crude.

:::

::::

::::slide

![Historical archive image](/uploads/archive/pablo-fanques-circus-poster-february-8-1847.jpg)

:::hotspot{title="Pablo Fanque's Circus, Wakefield — 8 February 1847" x="89" y="9"}

Just five days on from the previous of Fanque's Circus dates we see another new poster design, and seemingly a new roster of acts and performers. For this date Fanque has put together a heavily equestrian bill.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Horses" x="12" y="15"}

Horses were a key characteristic of circuses for the first century or so of its existence. This might seem unusual now, but in the 1800s horses were an everyday sight for most people. Indeed, until steam power took over, horses were the main source of power for agriculture, mining and transport in the 19th century.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Ivan Mazepa" x="23" y="59"}

[Ivan Mazepa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivan_Mazepa) was a Ukrainian military hero who was the inspiration for Lord Byron's 1819 poem, _Mazeppa_. The poem helped to establish a legend that Mazepa was once tied to a horse as punishment for an affair with a nobleman's wife. It was enthusiastically received at the time, and inspired many other works and performances — including circus 'hippodramas' ('horse operas').

:::

:::hotspot{title="William Frederick Wallett" x="61" y="82"}

[William Frederick Wallett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._F._Wallett) was a popular circus clown and circus owner. Having been invited to perform for Queen Victoria in 1844, he referred to himself thereafter as 'the Queen's Jester'. In his [1870 autobiography](<https://www.google.co.uk/books/edition/The_Public_Life_of_W_F_Wallett_the_Queen/YfhzpDUfY_sC?hl=en>) Wallett described witnessing the tragic death of his friend Pablo Fanque's wife, Susannah Darby, in a Leeds circus collapse in 1848. Wallett's wife, Mary, had been sitting with Darby and escaped with minor injuries.

:::

:::hotspot{title="Jim Crow" x="23" y="85"}

Mr Kite's performance as Jim Crow is likely to have been based on a racist stereotype of enslaved black people in the United States, popularised by [Thomas D. Rice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_D._Rice). Rice brought his act to Britain in the 1830s, during which he would have performed in blackface. While today this would be considered offensive, Rice was so successful that it's said he returned to America wearing a vest with diamond-encrusted gold buttons. It's not known what Pablo Fanque, who was Black, thought of the popularity of this kind of act.

:::

:::hotspot{title="A marching band" x="80" y="97"}

This spectacle would presumably have seen Pablo Fanque, a skilled equestrianist, controlling 14 tethered horses from a carriage alongside a marching brass band.

:::

## What the early circus looked and smelled like

This excerpt from [**‘Beneath the Big Top: A Social History of the Circus in Britain’**](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Beneath-Big-Top-History-Britain/dp/1783030496), by [Dr. Steve Ward](https://steve-ward.weebly.com/), captures the sensations early circus audiences could expect:

_An enormous glittering chandelier, lit by hundreds of candles, is suspended above the circular ring and casts a warm glow on the proceedings. The first thing you notice is the smell – of burning tallow candles, of the sawdust in the ring, the odor of horses and the stink of the crowd packed tightly together._

_... The people in the upper gallery must have a marvelous view of that chandelier for the entry price of one shilling! It is hot up there and the heat from the candles and the crowd below gathers beneath the roof._

_The orchestra is seated between the front of the stage and the ring and are playing a merry tune. In the ring at this moment is a young woman astride three white horses. As they gallop in a circle she balances on one leg on the middle horse and her diaphanous costume billows out behind her. The clown in the middle of the ring laughs and claps._

## Light relief in dark times

When circuses first became popular, most audiences would have had few comparable experiences. Travel was only for the **very wealthy.** What little leisure time people got would have been restricted to the area where they lived.

Moreover, the general quality of life was poor by today’s standards. People were barely expected to live past the age of 40. Many didn’t even live that long, due to **disease, poor living conditions and limited medical care**.

So a visit to the circus was a chance to **leave behind daily worries** and be entertained by dazzling feats. When menageries started to merge with circuses, exotic animals added yet more excitement.

## A dangerous profession – and spectacle

Circus owners didn’t generally prioritise the safety of performers, workers and spectators. With animals, daring feats and temporary structures in the same space, there was a lot that could go wrong.

Here are just a few examples of times when tragedy struck.

:::collection-image{layout="float-right" zoom="true"}

![An illustration of the Yarmouth Suspension Bridge Collapse, showing a listing bridge and people falling into the river.](/uploads/archive/an-illustration-of-the-yarmouth-suspension-bridge-collapse-2.jpg)

Image courtesy of Norfolk County Council Library and Information Service

:::

:::::collection-carousel{kind="quotes" label="Tragedies in the circus ring"}

::::slide

> In May 1845 Cooke's Circus advertised that Mr. Nelson the clown, sitting in a tub, would be pulled along Yarmouth's River Bure by four geese.
>
> Hundreds of people gathered on a bridge to watch. The bridge collapsed, causing the deaths of 79 people - many of them children. The bridge was later assessed to have substandard welding that contributed to its collapse.
>
> Nobody was held criminally responsible for the deaths. Today a memorial marks the site of the tragedy.

::::

::::slide

> On March 18 1848 - little more than a year after the Pablo Fanque posters on this page were printed - his circus was performing in Leeds.
>
> A balcony in the circus structure collapsed under the weight of the crowd gathered for the performance. Falling debris struck Fanque's wife, Susannah, who was in the crowd below. She died instantly.

::::

::::slide

> Selina Powell was a well-known tightrope walker on the circus circuit in the 19th century.
>
> While performing in Birmingham on July 20, 1863, Powell fell from her rope and died from her injuries. It was later revealed she had been in the advanced stages of pregnancy.
>
> The outrage was such that Queen Victoria was moved to comment publicly on 'her personal feelings of horror' at the incident, and questions were asked in Parliament.
